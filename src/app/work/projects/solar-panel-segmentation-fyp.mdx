## Overview

This Final Year Project explores **self-supervised learning (SSL)** as a practical way to segment solar panels from satellite imagery when labels are scarce. The end goal is a workflow that learns useful visual representations from unlabeled data first, then adapts to segmentation with a smaller set of masks.

## Problem statement (why annotation is hard)

Pixel-level segmentation labels are expensive in satellite imagery because:

- **Small targets & scale variance**: panels can be tiny relative to the full image, and look different across zoom levels.
- **Ambiguous boundaries**: glare, shadows, roof textures, and compression artifacts blur edges.
- **Domain shift**: different sensors, seasons, locations, and lighting change the visual statistics.
- **Class imbalance**: most pixels are “not panel”, so naïve sampling under-trains the minority class.
- **Throughput constraints**: large images and tiling make “label more data” a slow solution.

## Initial approach (SimCLR + MoCo v2 variants)

I used a two-stage plan:

- **Stage 1**: pretrain an encoder on unlabeled satellite imagery (SSL)
- **Stage 2**: attach a segmentation head and fine-tune on a limited mask set

### SimCLR experiments

- Trained with **in-batch negatives**, which pushes toward large batch sizes.
- Tested augmentation policies (cropping, color jitter, blur) and learned quickly that **augmentations that work on ImageNet can be counterproductive** for satellite imagery.

### MoCo v2 variants

- Implemented **MoCo v2-style momentum encoder + queue** to reduce the need for huge batches.
- Experimented with queue/momentum/temperature settings and augmentation mixes.

## Failure analysis (what went wrong and why)

The early contrastive runs were informative but not production-friendly:

- **Resource limits**: stable contrastive training benefited from higher throughput (batches / resolution) than I consistently had.
- **Instability**: training was sensitive to augmentation choices; some settings learned shortcuts instead of robust features.
- **Satellite-specific negatives**: “negative pairs” are tricky when many tiles look similar (rooftops, roads, farmland), which can weaken the contrastive signal.
- **Downstream uncertainty**: improvements in contrastive loss didn’t reliably translate to better segmentation behavior without careful evaluation tooling.

## Pivot decision (why BYOL)

I pivoted to **BYOL** because it:

- Removes explicit negatives, reducing sensitivity to batch/queue dynamics.
- Tends to be **more stable** under tighter compute budgets.
- Lets me focus on representation quality and evaluation rather than constant tuning to prevent training collapse.

## Results (honest summary)

- BYOL training achieved a **clean, consistently decreasing loss curve**.
- I monitored representation health via feature statistics; the **standard deviation stayed lower than expected**, which suggests limited feature diversity (and potential partial collapse).
- Practically: it’s “learning something”, but it’s not yet at the level where I’m comfortable claiming strong downstream segmentation gains.

## Limitations

- **No headline metrics** yet: I’m avoiding unverifiable numbers until downstream segmentation evaluation is fully standardized.
- **Evaluation still evolving**: linear-probe and small-label fine-tuning protocols need to be tightened for fair comparisons.
- **Data pipeline tradeoffs**: tiling strategy and augmentations strongly affect what the model learns.

## What I learned (technical takeaways)

- How to implement and debug **SimCLR / MoCo v2 / BYOL** training loops in PyTorch.
- Why **augmentation design is a first-class engineering problem** for SSL (especially outside ImageNet-like data).
- How to instrument training beyond loss: collapse checks, feature distribution monitoring, and reproducibility.
- How to structure a realistic CV workflow: pretraining → evaluation → segmentation fine-tuning, with iterative failure analysis.
